How I got here

I kept following the problem past the frontend

Three stages, and the same habit driving all of them.

Chapter one

Learning the craft

I started out building interfaces, and I got lucky with where. Teams that took testing seriously, products with real accessibility requirements, and enough scale that a bad release was somebody's actual problem. Component structure, types and tests became habits early instead of things I bolted on afterwards.

Chapter two

Hitting the boundary

As the products got bigger, the interesting problem was usually just past a line I wasn't meant to cross. A slow page that turned out to be a query. A confusing screen that was really a missing endpoint. I got tired of waiting for someone else to have time, so I learned enough to do it myself.

Chapter three

Owning services

Now I own six services instead of a set of screens. Design conversations, API contracts, code on both sides, tests, pipelines, and the escalation at nine at night when something breaks. Harder job. Much more interesting one.

How I work

Roughly the same four steps every time

Not a methodology. Just the order that stops me building the wrong thing well.

  1. Step one

    Understand the constraint

    Before any design work, find out what's actually driving the request. A compliance rule, a customer escalation, a cost, a limit someone hit. The feature people ask for and the problem behind it are often different things, and only one of them tells you when you're done.

  2. Step two

    Agree the contract

    Settle what sits between the pieces before anyone builds: what the API returns, who owns which behavior, what happens when it fails. Written down, agreed by both sides.

  3. Step three

    Build in thin slices

    Ship something small and real, get it in front of QA and whoever asked for it, adjust. Tests as I go. Leaving integration until the end has never once worked out for me.

  4. Step four

    Own it in production

    Verify it in the real environment, watch it for a while, write it up for whoever inherits it, and pick up the escalation when it comes.
